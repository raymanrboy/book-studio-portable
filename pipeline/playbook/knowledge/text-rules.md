# Text Rules

## Per-Spread Text Constraints

| Parameter | Rule |
|---|---|
| Lines per spread | 6 short lines (sometimes 8 is acceptable) |
| Line length & meter | **Uniform length:** 4-5 "full" words per line (strictly 7-8 syllables); forms a clean visual rectangle |
| Words per spread | ~25-35 words per spread |
| Total book word count | ~250–450 words (excluding dedication and finale) |
| Font implication | Large, readable — write for spoken aloud, not for reading silently; predictable Canva text box |

---

## Rhyme

Rhyme is welcome but not mandatory. When used:
- **AABB** (couplets) — safest, most natural for reading aloud
- **ABAB** — works for slower, more meditative formulas (B, E)
- Never force a rhyme at the cost of meaning or naturalness
- If a line sounds unnatural spoken aloud, rewrite it

One non-rhyming spread per book is fine. Two is the max.

## Poetry quality & American English (the Text Critic standard)

Write to this from the start (Agent 3); the Text Critic (Agent 4) enforces it.
**Audience: US native English speakers** — it must never sound translated, stiff, or "AI".

**Rhyme — smooth, predictable, and simple:**
- **Simple rhymes are best:** Use classic, basic rhymes (`today/play`, `sky/high`, `new/true`, `sweet/feet`). They make reading aloud much easier for parents. Do not try to use overly complex or clever rhymes.
- **Flawless Rhythm:** The meter must be perfect. It should read like a bouncing nursery rhyme (e.g., Mother Goose). If a parent would stumble reading it aloud at bedtime, rewrite it.
- **No filler crutches:** Do NOT force the meter by starting every other line with filler words like "And", "So", "Then", or "But". Keep lines natural and self-contained.
- No inverted or archaic word order.

**Meter & Uniform Line Length (The Canva Rectangle Standard):**
- Steady, singable, consistent within a stanza; read aloud — if it trips, rewrite.
- **Uniform visual width:** Every line in the stanza MUST be approximately the same length (4–5 full words, strictly 7–8 syllables).
- **No jagged lines or runaway tails:** Never pair a 3-word line with an 8-word line. Uneven line lengths break layout in Canva, cause awkward single-word wrapping, and force manual font resizing.
- **The "Rectangle" Test:** Visually, the 6-line poem must form a neat, balanced rectangular text block that drops effortlessly into the Canva text-safe area.

**American English:**
- US spelling (color, favorite, gray, traveled). US idiom; natural contractions a parent would use.
- NO Britishisms/archaisms: o'er, whilst, mum, ne'er, 'tis, thee, thou.
- NO AI-stock phrasing: "a tapestry of", "in a world of", "little did they know",
  "with each passing", "nestled in the heart of".

**Grammar:** correct, clean, natural. No run-ons, agreement errors, or odd punctuation.

## Craft Pattern for Strong Simple Stanzas

Strong text for these books should have short lines and a snappy rhythm. A perfect
6-line spread works like this:

1. **Lines 1-2 (Image & Action):** What we see and what it does.
2. **Lines 3-4 (Promise):** How it connects to love or safety.
3. **Lines 5-6 (Childward Turn & Finale):** Bringing the child (`{Name}`) into the scene gently.

Best-in-class example (perfect 6 lines, simple rhymes):
```
Morning sun is shining bright,
Little birds will take to flight.
Singing songs up in the tree,
Happy as a bird can be.
{Name}, a brand new day is here,
God is always standing near.
```

Why it works: very short lines (4-5 words max), predictable simple rhymes (bright/flight, tree/be, here/near), and flawless meter. Visually, it forms a perfect square text block.

Another good pattern (Ocean theme):
```
Little turtle in the sea,
Swimming happy, swimming free.
Waves are rolling to the shore,
Bringing shells and something more.
{Name}, the world is wide and deep,
Safe and quiet while you sleep.
```

Why it works: smooth rhythm, zero filler words like "And" at the start of lines, and the placeholder `{Name}` safely placed at the beginning of the line.

**Avoid these weak patterns:**
- Reusing the same image family too often (flowers, stars, sleep, rest, nest) unless repetition is
  intentional and emotionally building.
- Two pretty lines that do not connect. The nature image must bridge into the promise.
- Awkward mouthfeel. If a native parent would stumble reading it aloud, rewrite it.
- Forcing `{Name}` into the strongest line if it weakens the rhythm; place `{Name}` at a natural
  promise/comfort hinge instead.
- Rhyme that says less than the non-rhyming version would have said.

## {Name} Insertion Rules

- `{Name}` is the placeholder for the child's name (e.g., "Stella", "Liam").
- Must appear in the text at least once every 2–3 spreads.
- **CRITICAL HARD GATE — NEVER RHYME ON {Name}:** `{Name}` MUST NEVER be placed as the last word of a rhyming line. Since the actual name is unknown at generation time (e.g., could be "Alexander"), placing it in a rhyming position (e.g., trying to rhyme `{Name}` with "game" or "same") will completely destroy the rhyme in the final product. Always place `{Name}` at the START of the line, or safely embedded in the MIDDLE of the line, far from the rhyming end.
- **CRITICAL HARD GATE — DIVERSIFY {Name} PLACEMENT & LINES:** Do NOT place `{Name}` in the exact same line or position in every spread (e.g., do NOT mechanically put `{Name}` at the start of Line 5 or Line 7 across the whole book). Vary the line number (Line 2, Line 3, Line 5, Line 6, Line 7) and vary the placement within the line (beginning, middle, inside a clause).
- **Name Variety (Grammatical Roles):** Do not use `{Name}` ONLY as a direct vocative/address (e.g., "{Name}, you are loved"). Weave it into the story in different roles:
  - **As an observer / possessive:** `"God made the sun for {Name}'s eyes"`, `"Where {Name} watches ripples go"`
  - **As an active subject:** `"And when {Name} steps out to try"`, `"When {Name} stops to rest and play"`
  - **As a receiver of action/care:** `"God watches over {Name}'s way"`, `"Water dances near {Name}'s feet"`, `"God keeps sweet {Name} safe and dear"`

Good placement examples (varied lines, middle or start of clause, never rhyming end):
```
God made the sun for {Name}'s eyes,   (Line 3, middle possessive)
And when {Name} steps out to try,     (Line 5, middle subject)
Water dances near {Name}'s feet,      (Line 3, middle object)
God keeps sweet {Name} safe and dear, (Line 7, middle receiver)
```

**ABSOLUTELY FORBIDDEN PLACEMENT** (Rhyming position):
```
Close your little eyes, dear {Name},
Tomorrow brings a joyful game.
```
*(If the real name is "Olivia", "Olivia" does not rhyme with "game"!).*

## Sensitive Words and Patterns

**Context rule — these words are NOT globally banned.**
Words like `fair`, `white`, `light`, `dark`, and `pale` are risky when they describe a
child's skin, beauty, worth, goodness, purity, innocence, or appearance. In that context they
can create racial ambiguity or racist readings, especially in personalized books where the
child's skin tone varies.

Use them freely when they clearly refer to ordinary non-person appearance or scenery:
`moonlight`, `morning light`, `white paper`, `dark sky`, `pale blue flowers`, `light rain`.
Do not over-sanitize natural language when the meaning is clearly environmental.

**Avoid in appearance/skin contexts:**
- `fair`, `fairest`, `white`, `whiteness`, `light`, `dark`, `pale`
- any use of `fair` meaning "beautiful" for a child or doll
- `dark` as a character trait or moral/emotional descriptor

**Comparison patterns:**
- Never compare the child's appearance to another character
- Never say "as [adjective] as [noun]" if the noun implies physical appearance

**Negative emotional states with no resolution:**
- Do not end a spread on fear, sadness, or loneliness
- Exception: a "challenge" spread mid-book that resolves on the NEXT spread

**Gendered language:**
- Default to gender-neutral unless the listing explicitly targets a gender
- "she/her" only if listing says "for girl"; "he/him" only if "for boy"
- Safe default: use the child's name instead of pronouns where possible

## Voice and Register

- Speak TO the child, not ABOUT them: "You are brave, {Name}" not "She was very brave"
- Keep vocabulary simple: no word a 6-year-old wouldn't understand
- Warmth over cleverness — a cliché that lands emotionally beats a witty line that doesn't
- Each spread's text should work as a standalone if the parent quotes it on Instagram

## Dedication Template

```
Dear {Name},

[Line 1: occasion or moment — e.g., "On your very first birthday,"]
[Line 2: what this book is — e.g., "we made this book just for you."]
[Line 3: core wish — e.g., "May you always know how deeply loved you are."]

With love,
{Gifter}
```

## Finale Template

Finish with a closing wish. Last line should land hard.

Examples:
- "And wherever you go, {Name}, our love goes too."
- "You are the story we never want to end."
- "Sweet dreams, {Name} — the world is glad you're here."

## Afterword / Wishes Page (page 30 · separate square end image)

A warm closing page AFTER the finale scene — a short 2–4 line stanza or blessing, distinct from the finale stanza.
Center-safe. Weaves `{Name}` naturally into a memorable closing stanza.
Same house style as the Dedication so the first and last pages mirror each other.

Examples (4-line warmth standard):
```
From near or far, through every season,
our hearts belong to you.

Happy first birthday, dearest {Name} —
we're so very glad you're you.
```

```
From morning light to evening star,
God holds you close, right where you are.

On your baptism day and always, dear {Name} —
we're so very glad you're you.
```
